# Decisions

Why the system is built the way it is: each entry is a decision, what it was weighed against, and the evidence. The runs behind them were made between September and October 2026 on one story (the LEGO minifigure in a white void), with OpenAI models and LTX-2.5 on rented H100s. They are grounding for the design, not benchmarks.

> **Name changes (October 2026).** The Director became Spark, then **Now**; the Showrunner became Echo, then **Then**; the story blueprint (`story.json`) became **Frame Zero** (`frame_zero.json`); the adventure became the **story** (`story/`), a segment or turn a **clip**, an intervention, line or request an **input**, a turning point or era a **checkpoint**, and a continuation the **saved state**. The entries below use the current names.

## Orchestration

**Two LLM roles instead of four.** The first engine split every clip across four LLM roles. It was replaced by one Now call per beat and a Then on a slow clock, over a typed world model that only code mutates. In an A/B over 30 viewer turns the new engine took 178 s and 39 calls against 265 s and 105 calls, and its prop ledger stayed correct where the old one's was wrong on 22 of 30 turns. A 180-clip run afterwards had no failures, tracked 40 props, paid off 42 of 42 story threads and kept the prompt context bounded at about 2,400 words.

**Then never blocks the film.** The film used to wait for Then at the next clip. In a live session that and a slow Now call held the picture for 45 s over 27 clip transitions; Then join alone stalled 2-8 s on a fifth of the clips. Now its update lands at the first plan that finds it finished, Now writes less (it ran on a priority tier then, dropped since: luna's replies are short), and dry runs held the picture for 0.1 s over 23 transitions.

**Beats, rendered just in time.** Now writes one beat (one action or state, 1-4 clips) per call and the clips render with no LLM call between them. Each render fires its estimate plus a buffer (4.7 s) before its clip is due, and a viewer's line cuts the beat short and restarts Now call. A deeper queue alone made playback smooth but pushed an input's effect from two or three clips out to four; rendering just in time keeps both, and an input typed during clip N lands at N+2. Starting the next beat's call as soon as the last clip is out helps with a fast Now and is neutral with a slow one. Admitting inputs against a deadline to shrink the buffer traded stalls for latency one for one in simulation and was dropped.

**The viewer outranks the scene card.** Over 14 runs of 40 clips, Now drifted back to Then's scene card after 4 of 9 viewer inputs. A direction then stayed active for 90 s of film or until the next input, only a card planned after the input cleared it, and the card was paused for Now meanwhile; the expiry was lost in a later rewrite, and a direction now ends when Now says so (below). Removing Then's planning altogether stopped the snap-backs, but once the viewer went quiet the film barely moved (0.38 structural changes per minute against 1.15, and 151 s without one), so the planning stayed.

**Suggestions, not commands; characters who want things; a film that moves on its own.** In the last four headless films Now absorbed 3 of 3 viewer lines as written ("exactly as requested"), the Spaceman never went after his flag in either film he was in, no newcomer appeared, and a 119-clip film with no viewer had one prop and six scene cards about it. Its rules asked every new prop for a reason and every possible request to be carried out. Now a line is a suggestion Now must take up in the story's own way (a twist, a character who resists or does it its own way, a consequence that pays off later), characters act from what they want, and both roles are asked to keep the story progressing instead of needing a reason for each new thing. Not yet measured in a film.

**No code measures pacing.** The engine used to score stagnation (similarity of recent clips, a ladder of nudges, a forced scene end). In one film it reported no pacing pressure on every beat of a ball-passing loop, because bookkeeping changes reset its clock. Both roles now see how long the scene has run and judge for themselves whether the film is going in circles; Then checks on every run.

**One line of prose per entity.** Characters and props carried structured fields (holder, posture, activity, position, status). They drifted apart, invited misreadings (a model wrote `holder: "trumpet"`), and a holder flip counted as a story change on 49 of 119 clips. Each entity now has one `state` sentence, replaced whole when it changes. A 60-clip check had no warnings or retries.

**Personality holds, behavior moves; nobody is the protagonist.** Frame Zero's character was still built unlike a newcomer (a flag left from the protected-protagonist cast, its personality under another field name, no voice), a portrait lived only in the notebook, so a character Then dropped came back with the personality it started with, and an op giving an off-screen character a location dragged the camera, and everything in the picture, there. Now every character carries its `personality` and how it has been `lately` on its entity, Then leaves the personality empty to keep it (a rewrite on every run rewords it, and rewording drifts) and changes it only with a `because`, the camera moves only with what is in the picture, a cut no op moves lands where its shot evidently is, and both roles are told the story follows whoever it is most alive around. An absent character is recalled in full, with the summary of when it was last seen, only when the story points at it (left where the picture is, or named, or its place named, in what the roles read), so a return is written knowing who comes back without every absent character in every prompt; a return nobody named still gets its full entry a beat late. Not yet measured in a film.

**The viewer's memory belongs to the person, not the world.** Then's picture of the viewer was one paragraph in the notebook, rewritten every run from its previous version and the last 12 inputs, so a guess made once was read back a minute later as established and older evidence was lost; the viewer's direction never expired, so one input kept bending a silent film; and it was rolled back with the world, which is part of it. The memory is now its own append-only file: code records each input with the clip on air and what the story did with it once that clip aired, Then's readings must cite the inputs they rest on (code counts them for Now, so one input stays a guess), the viewer's marks are recorded once and found again by the entities they touch, Now closes a direction when it is fulfilled or has run its course, and every record carries its clip. No attention signal is recorded: silence stays unknown. Not yet measured in a film.

**One story, saved as of the last clip the viewer saw, without its video.** Every run began a new world; now each viewer has one story in a local folder, committed as each clip goes on air (the world as of that clip, renamed into place, then the clip appended to its log) and resumed from the newest commit. The video is not kept: a clip is 0.7-2 MB (about 1.1 GB per hour of film), but LTX-2.5 continues from exactly one thing, the previous clip's last 9 frames (diffusers trims a frame condition to 8k+1 frames and does not re-compress a multi-frame one; windows of 1, 9 and 17 landed a steered action at the same frame, and restoring the window from the client's copy kept the scene through a server restart, seam 1.66 against 66.1 for a re-seed). Those 9 frames cut losslessly from the aired clip are 112-275 KB and decode to exactly the frames that restore would use. Reading the server showed that a reference image on an existing session kept its old window and ignored a client's frames, so a second run against a server that stayed up would continue the earlier film's picture; a client's first request now starts a fresh session from either the reference or the saved frames. Offline, with the model calls stubbed, a new story, a resume, a SIGKILL mid-film and every crash point of a commit all came back to the last aired clip with a consistent clip log, and a resume against a fake server sent the 9 saved frames and no reference. Not yet run on a GPU or with real models.

**Prompts written for any story, not the void.** The prompts had grown around the LEGO void: something new meant a new prop, the examples were the story's own, and fields nothing used stayed. In the films since the prompts last changed (57 beats, 24 Then runs): a beat's perspective was set once in 1,369 logged beats; the scene card's intensity was never "high" and no code read it; threads were the current bit's small gags (a median of 3 clips from open to payoff, none ever dropped); the arc's current act moved in 2 of 15 films; and entity ops were 41 % of Now's output against 20 % for the picture itself, mostly a character's pose restating the clip's end image. Perspective and intensity are gone; something new is an arrival, an obstacle, a discovery or a turn; a thread is a setup the story comes back to; Then is told when to move to the next act; and a character's op is for a lasting change, not every pose. Not yet measured in a film. Dropping "most clips are silent" was undone: in two films of the quest story a line was spoken in 65 % of clips, with two or more speakers in 70 of 200, and the story's own "a line now and then" changed nothing; with the default back (the story may override it) and one speaker per clip, a check film had a line in 40 of 100 clips and never two speakers. Two speakers in one clip then rendered as two distinct voices on the GPU (one scripted clip, judged by ear), so the one-speaker rule was dropped again.

**Frame Zero is the first frame, not the world.** It held the story's truths, its characters' wants and natures, a style paragraph and a one-sentence visual anchor. The style sat in Now's system prompt on every call ("the world is white and empty"), the anchor opened every clip's video prompt ("no colour, no background"), and a place Now made had its look dropped, so it was never named to the video model: no story could leave the white on screen. Now Frame Zero is a scene sentence, one character (name, look, starting personality, position), one place (name, look) and the reference image; all of it becomes ordinary world state, the personality is shown to the roles until Then writes the character's portrait, a new place is drawn from its look, a place's look can change, and the style lives in the looks. Rules for every story are a separate content policy in both system prompts. Every shortened anchor let details in by clip 12 on the LEGO figure (below), so whether the looks alone hold the stick figure's style is not yet checked on the GPU.

**One story on main; the map on a branch.** The story map (checkpoints the viewer can go back to, branches and a map page as the front door) was built offline in October and never played with real models. Main is the version to show people, so it plays one story and nothing else: the film page opens on the picture the story stopped on, Begin or Continue starts a film, Stop or 60 s without a page open ends it, saved as of the clip on air, and the next film continues it. Only the newest saved state is kept. The map, branches and the ring of recent moments it needs are on the `story-map` branch (from c5ac66a), to be ported onto main later rather than merged. Offline (stubbed models, fake renderer): a new story, an input, Stop and Continue resumed at the next clip; a page closed mid-film stopped it after the wait; a kill -9 left the clip log whole and resumed at the last aired clip; Continue on video started the renderer from the 9 saved frames; `adopt` took a copy ahead and refused one behind.

**The story remembers in levels, and what comes back brings its past.** Six hours in, the roles saw the opening summary, one act paragraph Then overwrote whenever it judged the phase had changed, the last few summaries and the summary of when each recalled character was last seen; about 55 summaries an hour were stored and never read, and Then's prompt promised "a history it can look back into" that it could not see. That is how an infinite story repeats itself and forgets what it planted. Now Then also keeps the story since the last checkpoint (one paragraph, rewritten each run) and, at each checkpoint it names, rewrites the story so far (the old one with the new stretch folded in), so the whole story is covered in a bounded number of words; every summary and checkpoint keeps the entities it involves, and something in play that the role's recent summaries do not hold brings the checkpoint and summary it was last part of. No embeddings and no extra call: retrieval matches names, as recall did. The records moved out of the world into `history.jsonl`, so the world saved and dumped with every clip stays small (it had grown by about 33 KB per film-hour), and a clip that never airs is rolled back from the JSON its predecessor's commit wrote instead of a deep copy. How often a real Then names a checkpoint is not yet measured.

**The cast is in view, nothing owed is forgotten, and a mark can change.** An absent character was an id with its last state and place, so a woman in a red coat last seen an hour ago was invisible as a person and Now invented "a stranger in a red coat"; a removed character was listed nowhere and its name was burned (introducing it again only updated the gone one); an owed input stayed owed until delivered, only the four newest were shown, two owed at one clip could not be told apart, and one owed input plus any absorbed beat counted as delivered by a hidden rule; a viewer who renamed the dog left two permanent marks. Now an absent character's line says who it is and how it was lately, dated; the gone are listed with how they left, an update with `present: true` brings one back, and a name of theirs introduced again is someone new (exact ids still name the gone one); every owed input is shown with its id and age, and Now resolves each by id, delivered (it becomes the direction) or let go, with nothing expiring on a timer; a mark may replace an earlier one; seeds and readings are kept to the eight and six Then is told; both prompts ask for a few characters who grow; and Now's prompt stops naming a `who` field, saying gone things are listed when they were not, and calling a beat "played out" when its last clip was only written. Mac now comes with what he wants most and a voice. Checked offline on built worlds; not yet in a film.

**A failed Now call stops the film; code never writes a clip.** A Now call had no timeout of its own (the SDK's 600 s and two retries), and one that failed was replaced by a clip code wrote ("Mac holds still"), saved as story like any other; a failing first Then run was retried before every beat; stopping a film left a Now call running and billing; a failed save was only logged; and a film that stopped on an error told the page nothing. Now has 20 s per call (its median is 6.5 s, and past about 10 s the picture holds anyway) and one more try, after which the film stops, plays out what is rendered and says why; Then has 90 s and two retries, its opening run one try; both replies may run to 4,096 tokens (the largest seen were about 1,300), since a reply cut off is lost whole; three failed saves in a row stop the film. Offline (a fake OpenAI server, so the real client's timeouts and errors were exercised): one failure was retried and the film went on; two 500s, a cut-off reply, a refusal, a revoked key and a call that hung each stopped the film with the reason (the hang after 2.3 s at a 1 s timeout); a Stop during a hanging call dropped it at once; a film stopped before its first clip aired left the owed input unanswered, and the next film recorded it deferred with its first clip.

**gpt-5.6-luna for both roles, no reasoning.** 24 Now configurations were compared on 71 beats captured from real films, and Thens on 22 cases, judged blind in both orders by two judge families, plus 88 multi-step films and three live films. luna was the only Now to beat gpt-5.4-mini under both judges (win share 0.62 and 0.55) and made the best films; as Then it won 22 of 22 against gpt-4.1. It costs $0.50 per film-hour against $3.88 before. No self-hostable open model directed as well (the best won 35-41 % head to head), and sharing the render GPU with an LLM raised the render p95 by 36 %.

## Picture and sound

**The drift was the sampling recipe, not the prompts.** Early films drifted within each clip (faces, torsos, a greying ground), and a week of prompt work did not fix it. The distilled transformer was being sampled with the full model's recipe (15 steps, CFG 3, STG). On its own recipe (8 sigmas, unguided) the figure holds through the action. The full model on its recipe gave the same identity at 3.5× the time and added an extra character. Remaining "drift" was facial expressions Now wrote; the LEGO story now forbids them.

**No identity anchor.** Conditioning every clip on the reference image as an extra keyframe suppressed the clip's closing action when placed at the end and inserted an idle beat when placed in the middle. On the correct recipe it is not needed.

**No re-seeding mid-film.** A cut re-seeded from the reference image resets everything the story changed: a cap picked up mid-film vanished, and a re-entry put a second figure on screen. Only a film's first clip is seeded from the reference; every other clip continues from the last 9 frames of the one before. After a server restart the client sends that tail, and the scene survives (a frame difference at the seam of 1.7, against 66 with a re-seed).

**Cuts are allowed, sparingly.** LTX-2.5 follows a written cut ("The view cuts to …"): 6 of 6 requested cuts happened and none of 12 unrequested. But the cut lands 2-4 s into the clip, a new angle renders as the figure turning, close-ups repaint the face, and some cuts dissolve. The LEGO story was one take; since every story may travel anywhere, and a walk does not reach a new place on screen (below), every story may now cut. In the first text film of a story with cuts on, Now cut on 42 of 100 clips, mostly to a new angle; it is now told what a cut costs the render, and to cut only to another location or for a reveal.

**A place is drawn from where the protagonist is.** A location with a look is named in every clip's prompt (in full on the clip the picture arrives there, short while it stays), and the scene follows the protagonist: what is in the picture comes along, the rest stays where it was left. In the first 200-clip film of the quest story Now moved the location 37 clips after the action entered the tower, so every prompt in between said "mountain pass" inside the tower. Told that the location is what the video model draws, and shown it in World now, it moved the location on the clip the picture arrived in a check film. On the GPU (an H200 NVL, one scripted chain and one live 120-clip film) a cut to a new place landed its look within 2.5 s, while a walk kept drawing the old place: one dissolved to near-black and the figure came back with a hair piece, another made the dragon's tower a beige castle hall. A story with cuts now arrives by cutting; one without cuts travels poorly. Wide landscape shots went photo-real; the story's style now says everything, landscape and sky included, is LEGO (not yet checked on the GPU). The H200 renders a 6 s clip in 2.8 s (p95 3.1 s), faster than the H100.

**No frame observer.** A vision model checked every rendered clip and could force a cut on drift. After the recipe fix it measured drift that was gone, its forced cut never fired in any run, and it misread framing. Removed.

**One labelled prompt compiler.** Caption-style prose prompts held identity no better and ran 22 % longer. Every shortened version of the visual anchor let hair pieces or printed shirts in by clip 12 on 2 of 3 seeds; the full anchor did on none. The compiler sent the full style and looks on the opening clip and a short anchor afterwards; since Frame Zero stopped carrying a style it sends looks only (see the Frame Zero entry).

**A prop keeps its look in every clip, and what a character wears is on screen.** A prop was described on the clip it entered and named alone afterwards, and the video model redrew it: a red lion shield turned grey, into a wooden door or a bronze disc within a few clips. In a scripted 14-clip chain on two seeds (the same seeds, only the prompt different), restating each prop's short look kept it recognisable in 43 of 54 checks against 22, and missing in 6 against 22. Now also left worn and carried things out of `on_screen`, so the prompt never named them: a worn helmet was listed in 3 and 7 of 37 clips (28-64) in two live films, and Lego Man's own look ("bare yellow head") took it off. Told that the video model draws only what the prompt names, Now listed it in 36 of those 37 and in 122 of the 123 clips of its life; the helmet stayed a helmet, but the picture put it on whichever figure was there (the dragon wore it from clip 125), because the prompt does not say who wears it. Characters still drift a little with their short look (a hair piece or hat in 7-9 % of clips), and wide shots still go photo-real; colour does not drift over 180 clips.

**A costume changes the wearer's look.** In a scripted chain with two figures (a herald hands Lego Man a helmet; two seeds, 22 clips each way), Lego Man wore it in 8 of 22 clips and the herald took it in 14, while every prompt still gave Lego Man a "bare yellow head". Saying where the helmet was ("worn on Lego Man's head") changed nothing (9 of 22), and every full look on every clip made it worse (2 of 22: the full look adds "no hair piece"); longer is not better, a look that agrees with the costume is. An op may now replace a character's or prop's look whole when its appearance changes for good: with the helmet written into his look Lego Man wore it in 20 of 22, but the herald grew a copy in 18 of 22, and keeping the herald's own cap in his look did not stop that. In a text film Now wrote the new look when the helmet went on and the plain one back when a goat took it off; it also shortened the goat's look unasked, so it is told to leave `description` empty unless the look itself changes.

**512×288, one clip length.** One frame size, exactly 16:9, for the render and the reference image: identity and detail held by eye against 640×352 at 31 % less render time. One clip length (6 s), because under fp8 every new shape compiles for 90-170 s; the server warms the client's shape at startup and the client refuses to start without it.

**The video model renders all the sound.** LTX-2.5 renders ambient sound and the characters' lines from the prompt; an external narrator and TTS were removed, so every spoken word is a character's. Each clip renders 9 extra frames that repeat the previous clip's tail; the page cuts on them and crossfades the sound, with every clip held under -18 LUFS (no duplicate frames, no luminance dip). The model's own silences between clips still make audible steps; accepted. Two speakers in one clip render as two distinct voices (one scripted clip on an H200, judged by ear).

**No film is saved.** Each run used to join its played clips into one film (each clip's repeated 9-frame head shown once, the sound crossfaded: a 24-clip film saved exactly the 3,465 frames that played). Since the story is never replayed and the story folder keeps no video (above), clips are deleted once they have played and nothing is joined.

## Real time

The transformer's forward passes are compute-bound, so the levers were the GPU, low-precision matmuls, compilation and fewer tokens:

- fp8 matmuls (torchao, dynamic) and regional `torch.compile`: 3.0× to 1.9× real time at 960×544 on an H100.
- Fewer tokens: 640×352, then 512×288 (31 % faster again).
- Frames converted on the GPU and piped to ffmpeg as YUV with 8 x264 threads: render p95 0.89 to 0.67 of the clip's length.
- The client on the GPU box: an MP4 through an ssh tunnel cost 1.5-4.6 s per clip.

What did not help: the full model (3.5× slower, no better), FlashAttention-3 (refuses LTX-2's attention masks), NVENC (unavailable in Vast containers), offloading (28-57 s per clip). A 300-clip film showed no render creep; its three stalls all came from Now calls of 6-14 s, which remain the main risk to a seamless film.

## Known open problems

- **Long films drift to a floor.** As props accumulate, the white void turns into a LEGO baseplate.
- **Continuity against steering.** The sliding window trades one for the other (`ltxv_continuity_strength`); a better mechanism is needed, not a better setting.
- **Now latency tail.** A slow call at a beat's end or on a line can still hold the picture.
