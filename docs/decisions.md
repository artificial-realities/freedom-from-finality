# Decisions

Why the system is built the way it is: each entry is a decision, what it was weighed against, and the evidence. The runs behind them were made between September and October 2026, most of them on LEGO stories (a minifigure in a white void, then a quest) and the latest on Mac's story as text, with OpenAI models and LTX-2.5 on rented H100s and H200s. They are grounding for the design, not benchmarks.

> **Name changes (October and November 2026).** The Director became Spark, then **Now**, then **Goldfish**; the Showrunner became Echo, then **Then**, then **Elephant**; the story blueprint (`story.json`) became **Frame Zero** (`frame_zero.json`); the adventure became the **story** (`story/`), a segment or turn a **clip**, an intervention, line or request an **input**, a turning point or era a **checkpoint**, and a continuation the **saved state**. The entries below use the current names.

## Orchestration

**Two LLM roles instead of four.** The first engine split every clip across four LLM roles. It was replaced by one Goldfish call per beat and an Elephant on a slow clock, over a typed world model that only code mutates. In an A/B over 30 viewer turns the new engine took 178 s and 39 calls against 265 s and 105 calls, and its prop ledger stayed correct where the old one's was wrong on 22 of 30 turns. A 180-clip run afterwards had no failures, tracked 40 props, paid off 42 of 42 story threads and kept the prompt context bounded at about 2,400 words.

**Elephant never blocks the film.** The film used to wait for Elephant at the next clip. In a live session that and a slow Goldfish call held the picture for 45 s over 27 clip transitions; Elephant join alone stalled 2-8 s on a fifth of the clips. Now its update lands at the first plan that finds it finished, Goldfish writes less (it ran on a priority tier then, dropped since: luna's replies are short), and dry runs held the picture for 0.1 s over 23 transitions.

**Beats, rendered just in time.** Goldfish writes one beat (one action or state, 1-4 clips) per call and the clips render with no LLM call between them. Each render fires its estimate plus a buffer (4.7 s) before its clip is due, and a viewer's line cuts the beat short and restarts Goldfish call. A deeper queue alone made playback smooth but pushed an input's effect from two or three clips out to four; rendering just in time keeps both, and an input typed during clip N lands at N+2. Starting the next beat's call as soon as the last clip is out helps with a fast Goldfish and is neutral with a slow one. Admitting inputs against a deadline to shrink the buffer traded stalls for latency one for one in simulation and was dropped.

**The viewer outranks the scene card.** Over 14 runs of 40 clips, Goldfish drifted back to Elephant's scene card after 4 of 9 viewer inputs. A direction then stayed active for 90 s of film or until the next input, only a card planned after the input cleared it, and the card was paused for Goldfish meanwhile; the expiry was lost in a later rewrite, and a direction now ends when Goldfish says so (below). Removing Elephant's planning altogether stopped the snap-backs, but once the viewer went quiet the film barely moved (0.38 structural changes per minute against 1.15, and 151 s without one), so the planning stayed.

**Suggestions, not commands; characters who want things; a film that moves on its own.** In the last four headless films Goldfish absorbed 3 of 3 viewer lines as written ("exactly as requested"), the Spaceman never went after his flag in either film he was in, no newcomer appeared, and a 119-clip film with no viewer had one prop and six scene cards about it. Its rules asked every new prop for a reason and every possible request to be carried out. Now a line is a suggestion Goldfish must take up in the story's own way (a twist, a character who resists or does it its own way, a consequence that pays off later), characters act from what they want, and both roles are asked to keep the story progressing instead of needing a reason for each new thing. Not yet measured in a film.

**No code measures pacing.** The engine used to score stagnation (similarity of recent clips, a ladder of nudges, a forced scene end). In one film it reported no pacing pressure on every beat of a ball-passing loop, because bookkeeping changes reset its clock. Both roles now see how long the scene has run and judge for themselves whether the film is going in circles; Elephant checks on every run.

**One line of prose per entity.** Characters and props carried structured fields (holder, posture, activity, position, status). They drifted apart, invited misreadings (a model wrote `holder: "trumpet"`), and a holder flip counted as a story change on 49 of 119 clips. Each entity now has one `state` sentence, replaced whole when it changes. A 60-clip check had no warnings or retries.

**Personality holds, behavior moves; nobody is the protagonist.** Frame Zero's character was still built unlike a newcomer (a flag left from the protected-protagonist cast, its personality under another field name, no voice), a portrait lived only in the notebook, so a character Elephant dropped came back with the personality it started with, and an op giving an off-screen character a location dragged the camera, and everything in the picture, there. Now every character carries its `personality` and how it has been `lately` on its entity, Elephant leaves the personality empty to keep it (a rewrite on every run rewords it, and rewording drifts) and changes it only with a `because`, the camera moves only with what is in the picture, a cut no op moves lands where its shot evidently is, and both roles are told the story follows whoever it is most alive around. An absent character is recalled in full, with the summary of when it was last seen, only when the story points at it (left where the picture is, or named, or its place named, in what the roles read), so a return is written knowing who comes back without every absent character in every prompt; a return nobody named still gets its full entry a beat late. Not yet measured in a film.

**The viewer's memory belongs to the person, not the world.** Elephant's picture of the viewer was one paragraph in the notebook, rewritten every run from its previous version and the last 12 inputs, so a guess made once was read back a minute later as established and older evidence was lost; the viewer's direction never expired, so one input kept bending a silent film; and it was rolled back with the world, which is part of it. The memory is now its own append-only file: code records each input with the clip on air and what the story did with it once that clip aired, Elephant's readings must cite the inputs they rest on (code counts them for Goldfish, so one input stays a guess), the viewer's marks are recorded once and found again by the entities they touch, Goldfish closes a direction when it is fulfilled or has run its course, and every record carries its clip. No attention signal is recorded: silence stays unknown. Not yet measured in a film.

**One story, saved as of the last clip the viewer saw, without its video.** Every run began a new world; now each viewer has one story in a local folder, committed as each clip goes on air (the world as of that clip, renamed into place, then the clip appended to its log) and resumed from the newest commit. The video is not kept: a clip is 0.7-2 MB (about 1.1 GB per hour of film), but LTX-2.5 continues from exactly one thing, the previous clip's last 9 frames (diffusers trims a frame condition to 8k+1 frames and does not re-compress a multi-frame one; windows of 1, 9 and 17 landed a steered action at the same frame, and restoring the window from the client's copy kept the scene through a server restart, seam 1.66 against 66.1 for a re-seed). Those 9 frames cut losslessly from the aired clip are 112-275 KB and decode to exactly the frames that restore would use. Reading the server showed that a reference image on an existing session kept its old window and ignored a client's frames, so a second run against a server that stayed up would continue the earlier film's picture; a client's first request now starts a fresh session from either the reference or the saved frames. Offline, with the model calls stubbed, a new story, a resume, a SIGKILL mid-film and every crash point of a commit all came back to the last aired clip with a consistent clip log, and a resume against a fake server sent the 9 saved frames and no reference. Not yet run on a GPU or with real models.

**Prompts written for any story, not the void.** The prompts had grown around the LEGO void: something new meant a new prop, the examples were the story's own, and fields nothing used stayed. In the films since the prompts last changed (57 beats, 24 Elephant runs): a beat's perspective was set once in 1,369 logged beats; the scene card's intensity was never "high" and no code read it; threads were the current bit's small gags (a median of 3 clips from open to payoff, none ever dropped); the arc's current act moved in 2 of 15 films; and entity ops were 41 % of Goldfish's output against 20 % for the picture itself, mostly a character's pose restating the clip's end image. Perspective and intensity are gone; something new is an arrival, an obstacle, a discovery or a turn; a thread is a setup the story comes back to; Elephant is told when to move to the next act; and a character's op is for a lasting change, not every pose. Not yet measured in a film. Dropping "most clips are silent" was undone: in two films of the quest story a line was spoken in 65 % of clips, with two or more speakers in 70 of 200, and the story's own "a line now and then" changed nothing; with the default back (the story may override it) and one speaker per clip, a check film had a line in 40 of 100 clips and never two speakers. Two speakers in one clip then rendered as two distinct voices on the GPU (one scripted clip, judged by ear), so the one-speaker rule was dropped again.

**Frame Zero is the first frame, not the world.** It held the story's truths, its characters' wants and natures, a style paragraph and a one-sentence visual anchor. The style sat in Goldfish's system prompt on every call ("the world is white and empty"), the anchor opened every clip's video prompt ("no colour, no background"), and a place Goldfish made had its look dropped, so it was never named to the video model: no story could leave the white on screen. Now Frame Zero is a scene sentence, one character (name, look, starting personality, position), one place (name, look) and the reference image; all of it becomes ordinary world state, the personality is shown to the roles until Elephant writes the character's portrait, a new place is drawn from its look, a place's look can change, and the style lives in the looks. Rules for every story are a separate content policy in both system prompts. Every shortened anchor let details in by clip 12 on the LEGO figure (below), so whether the looks alone hold the stick figure's style is not yet checked on the GPU.

**One story on main; the map on a branch.** The story map (checkpoints the viewer can go back to, branches and a map page as the front door) was built offline in October and never played with real models. Main is the version to show people, so it plays one story and nothing else: the film page opens on the picture the story stopped on, Begin or Continue starts a film, Stop or 60 s without a page open ends it, saved as of the clip on air, and the next film continues it. Only the newest saved state is kept. The map, branches and the ring of recent moments it needs are on the `story-map` branch (from c5ac66a), to be ported onto main later rather than merged. Offline (stubbed models, fake renderer): a new story, an input, Stop and Continue resumed at the next clip; a page closed mid-film stopped it after the wait; a kill -9 left the clip log whole and resumed at the last aired clip; Continue on video started the renderer from the 9 saved frames; `adopt` took a copy ahead and refused one behind.

**The first films of the new main: text, luna, nobody typing and a scripted viewer.** Film A, 300 clips with no input ($0.27): Mac was on screen in 81 % of clips (56 % in clips 151-200, never away more than 4 clips); two characters joined (0.7 per 100 clips), both stayed (the woman in 226 clips) and neither got a name; the story built a remembered kitchen out of props in The White, as Frame Zero's look invites, so no location was introduced and the cut-landing rule never fired (79 cuts, all inside The White). Beats ran 2, 3 or 4 clips in 10, 89 and 1 % of 136; Goldfish's median call took 5.9 s (p95 12.1 s, 8 over 10 s), its largest reply was 1,594 tokens and its prompt 9,100 (72 % cached); Elephant's median run took 12.2 s, its largest reply 1,820 tokens, so both 4,096 caps hold with room. Film B, 200 clips with 13 scripted inputs and a Stop and Continue ($0.16, $0.49 per film-hour): every input reached the screen within 1-2 clips; the input typed just before Stop was recorded deferred with the first clip after Continue and delivered by its beat; "No, call the dog Biscuit instead" became a mark replacing "Rex"; a request for gore was declined and turned to non-graphic eeriness; "Go left." and "No, go right." were planned as one input; "Later, once it gets dark, let it snow" was taken up at once rather than deferred; Goldfish closed every direction as fulfilled within 2-8 clips of taking it up. Two faults showed. Elephant named a checkpoint on every run (24 of 24 and 17 of 17), several retelling the story from its start, so the story so far was rewritten every run; its prompt now says a turn is rarer than a scene or a revelation (not while the story is one stretch, the same people wanting the same things and facing the same question), that a checkpoint summarises only the stretch it closes, and how often it has named them. And a name given later never reached the entity (the dog stayed "small dog", so "Biscuit" named nothing); an update may now rename an entity, and its earlier names still find it. Film C, 150 clips after both changes ($0.09): the dog became Biscuit (once small dog, Rex); Elephant named no checkpoint in 13 runs, each time saying why ("one continuous mystery stretch: the meeting with the older dog deepens the existing question"), and the story since the beginning stayed at 88 words. Whether it names one in a story of hours is not yet measured. A voice written as one phrase ("quiet and a little unsure") was repeated beside the line's delivery ("quiet, uncertain, quiet and a little unsure"); the compiler now compares qualities without their joining "and", and Mac's voice is a list of qualities.

**Text films of Mac's story: The White pulls toward symbols, and what the roles were given against it.** Fourteen text films (about 2,200 clips, about $1.90) with nobody typing, a scripted viewer, or a person typing as they watched. With nobody typing every film became symbols answering symbols: doors (18 to 62 mentions per 100 clips), lines and dots, copies of Mac (a duplicate, an older and a younger Mac, a "delayed outline"), a stranger with a lantern in three of four films, and lines like "That is not a name. It is a claim."; nobody laughed in about 570 clips. Elephant's notebook wrote themes ("the boundary between being seen and being claimed"), which came back almost word for word as characters' lines, and it called every variation a development. The same prompts from another Frame Zero (an old woman at a rainy bus stop) told a plain drama in 60 clips (13 doors per 100), and Mac in The White with a concrete want (his flat and a hungry cat) gained stakes but kept the doors (60 per 100): the pull is The White's emptiness and Mac's want to know what the place is, which prompts soften and do not remove. Goldfish is now told that a story can circle without repeating an image, that a mystery is a clear question, that people talk plainly rather than in riddles, and that an input can be about the telling (its mood, its pace, whether the viewer can follow), often beside a wish about what happens; Elephant notes circling, and writes the notebook in what can be filmed rather than themes. Riddle-like lines fell from 8 to 3 of 20 (blind, one rater); a quiet film in The White still filled with copies of Mac. "This is getting heavy lol. Mac is starving..." had been answered with food and the eerie tone back within two clips, twice; with the telling spelled out, "can something silly happen?" kept a lighter voice for about 15 clips and "I'm lost" had Mac explain the situation to camera; Goldfish still closes most directions as fulfilled within one or two beats. Recall held: every character back after more than 15 clips away was written with its full entry, and the past it was last in came back when no recent summary held it; an input typed just before Stop was answered after Continue through whoever was there (the keeper it named had been left behind). Two bugs: an Elephant run that finished during a Goldfish call landed only after the clock was read, so another spawned 4-6 clips later (about half the runs in one film, also in October's film A); and an introduce's `name` was dropped (Goldfish writes `entity: umbrella_woman, name: Iris`). A character who said her name ("I'm Mara") got no rename; Goldfish is told a name given is an op. A character the viewer sent away never left, or came back within 8 clips; Goldfish is told a leaving holds long enough for the absence to matter, and Pip then stayed away from clip 33 to the end at 110, the story becoming Mac's search for her. Checkpoints had overshot the other way: 0 or 1 per 200 clips in every film, so the 150-word story since the beginning kept only its newest stretch (one film: 97 words for 200 clips, the newcomer's arrival and the town gone), and nothing else held what stayed in play the whole time, since only what comes back brings its past. Telling Elephant that the question is the one the characters face now changed nothing (none in 100 clips); telling it that the story since the last checkpoint must hold its whole stretch, so that when it cannot the newest part's first clip is a checkpoint, gave 4 in 200 and 3 in 110 clips in films that moved (one pair a clip apart), and still none in a quiet film in The White.

**What the viewer asks for lasts; the prompts say less.** Eleven text films (about 1,500 clips, about $1.35 with the replays below): five where a person typed every 8 clips as they watched, five with nobody typing, and one of 240 clips with four early inputs and then silence. With the viewer's input as a "direction" Goldfish closed, Goldfish closed 8 of 8 as fulfilled within 2-5 clips, and the story snapped back: Elephant's intentions led back to the mystery ("let the bell pull Mac and Mara out of the brief safety of the meal", written right after the viewer asked for the meal), a visitor sent away was at the door 13 clips later, "just tell him already" was answered with riddles, and Elephant credited the viewer with "still open to mystery" that nothing they said showed. The direction is gone: both roles see the viewer's latest four inputs with how long ago each came and how the story took it (Goldfish's `input_reason` now says what the input asked for, not only what the beat did), they outrank the notebook, Elephant runs again as soon as it may after an input, and the beat after an answer is told it continues that answer. "Keep it light and funny" at clip 8 then held to the end of two 120-clip films and the 240-clip one, which still moved through 15 places on its own; a neighbour sent home "for good" stayed away for the remaining 46 clips; "stay out of the White" held; a lost viewer got "the archive records people and things that arrive here" rather than a riddle. A moment the viewer asks for ("just sit and talk") still lasts only two or three clips before something interrupts it. In both system prompts the art stayed and the targeted lines became general ones written into it (most of the world is ordinary; a mystery's teller knows its answer; what has ended holds; a newcomer brings another want; what the viewer asks for lasts), and Goldfish's technical contract was tightened (Goldfish 4,421 to 3,938 words, Elephant about as long as before). A version cut to half the length was tried first: on 17 captured contexts it wrote beats as good, judged blind, with fewer unknown ids (2 against 5 of 34), but it read as a rule list rather than a storyteller's craft, and the restored text wrote the same on the replayed contexts; removing Elephant's seeds and intentions from 8 contexts changed the riddle-talk not at all, since it follows the world the story has set up, while "most of the world is ordinary" gave newcomers lives of their own (a suitcase with a sandwich; a neighbour worried about his flowerbeds). The first shortening lost the weight on line length (46 % of clips carried more than 18 words of speech in one film, against 5 % before); with the words a clip can hold given to Goldfish (about 2.5 a second) it was 10 % in a banter-heavy film and 0 % in a quiet one. With the art restored, nearly every clip carried a line (2 % without one in a 120-clip check film); a sentence on silence in Goldfish's craft brought that to 16 % with the same inputs, close to the 23 % before. Elephant's seeds now state one decided answer instead of a list of possibilities. Quiet films in The White stayed abstract in every version, and luna names the first woman Mara in most films.

**No word counts between the roles.** What one role wrote reached the other cut to a length: the story since the last checkpoint at 300 words and the story so far at 500 (Elephant rewrote the first from its own cut copy, so whatever fell past the cut was lost), an entity's state at 160 characters, a viewer's input at 200. The cuts are gone, and the prompts ask for a paragraph, a few paragraphs or a short sentence instead of a number of words. In a 120-clip check film the paragraph since the last checkpoint ran 55-136 words, the story so far 154-192 and a state a median of 13 words, about as before. Limits that come from the picture stay: what a clip can hold in speech, and the short look restated on every clip; so do the counts that keep a never-ending story's prompts bounded (characters listed, seeds, readings, summaries shown).

**The story remembers in levels, and what comes back brings its past.** Six hours in, the roles saw the opening summary, one act paragraph Elephant overwrote whenever it judged the phase had changed, the last few summaries and the summary of when each recalled character was last seen; about 55 summaries an hour were stored and never read, and Elephant's prompt promised "a history it can look back into" that it could not see. That is how an infinite story repeats itself and forgets what it planted. Now Elephant also keeps the story since the last checkpoint (one paragraph, rewritten each run) and, at each checkpoint it names, rewrites the story so far (the old one with the new stretch folded in), so the whole story is covered in a bounded number of words; every summary and checkpoint keeps the entities it involves, and something in play that the role's recent summaries do not hold brings the checkpoint and summary it was last part of. No embeddings and no extra call: retrieval matches names, as recall did. The records moved out of the world into `history.jsonl`, so the world saved and dumped with every clip stays small (it had grown by about 33 KB per film-hour), and a clip that never airs is rolled back from the JSON its predecessor's commit wrote instead of a deep copy. How often a real Elephant names a checkpoint is not yet measured.

**The cast is in view, nothing owed is forgotten, and a mark can change.** An absent character was an id with its last state and place, so a woman in a red coat last seen an hour ago was invisible as a person and Goldfish invented "a stranger in a red coat"; a removed character was listed nowhere and its name was burned (introducing it again only updated the gone one); an owed input stayed owed until delivered, only the four newest were shown, two owed at one clip could not be told apart, and one owed input plus any absorbed beat counted as delivered by a hidden rule; a viewer who renamed the dog left two permanent marks. Now an absent character's line says who it is and how it was lately, dated; the gone are listed with how they left, an update with `present: true` brings one back, and a name of theirs introduced again is someone new (exact ids still name the gone one); every owed input is shown with its id and age, and Goldfish resolves each by id, delivered (it becomes the direction) or let go, with nothing expiring on a timer; a mark may replace an earlier one; seeds and readings are kept to the eight and six Elephant is told; both prompts ask for a few characters who grow; and Goldfish's prompt stops naming a `who` field, saying gone things are listed when they were not, and calling a beat "played out" when its last clip was only written. Mac now comes with what he wants most and a voice. Checked offline on built worlds; not yet in a film.

**A failed Goldfish call stops the film; code never writes a clip.** A Goldfish call had no timeout of its own (the SDK's 600 s and two retries), and one that failed was replaced by a clip code wrote ("Mac holds still"), saved as story like any other; a failing first Elephant run was retried before every beat; stopping a film left a Goldfish call running and billing; a failed save was only logged; and a film that stopped on an error told the page nothing. Goldfish has 20 s per call (its median is 6.5 s, and past about 10 s the picture holds anyway) and one more try, after which the film stops, plays out what is rendered and says why; Elephant has 90 s and two retries, its opening run one try; both replies may run to 4,096 tokens (the largest seen were about 1,300), since a reply cut off is lost whole; three failed saves in a row stop the film. Offline (a fake OpenAI server, so the real client's timeouts and errors were exercised): one failure was retried and the film went on; two 500s, a cut-off reply, a refusal, a revoked key and a call that hung each stopped the film with the reason (the hang after 2.3 s at a 1 s timeout); a Stop during a hanging call dropped it at once; a film stopped before its first clip aired left the owed input unanswered, and the next film recorded it deferred with its first clip.

**gpt-5.6-luna for both roles, no reasoning.** 24 Goldfish configurations were compared on 71 beats captured from real films, and Thens on 22 cases, judged blind in both orders by two judge families, plus 88 multi-step films and three live films. luna was the only Goldfish to beat gpt-5.4-mini under both judges (win share 0.62 and 0.55) and made the best films; as Elephant it won 22 of 22 against gpt-4.1. It costs $0.50 per film-hour against $3.88 before. No self-hostable open model directed as well (the best won 35-41 % head to head), and sharing the render GPU with an LLM raised the render p95 by 36 %.

## Picture and sound

**The drift was the sampling recipe, not the prompts.** Early films drifted within each clip (faces, torsos, a greying ground), and a week of prompt work did not fix it. The distilled transformer was being sampled with the full model's recipe (15 steps, CFG 3, STG). On its own recipe (8 sigmas, unguided) the figure holds through the action. The full model on its recipe gave the same identity at 3.5× the time and added an extra character. Remaining "drift" was facial expressions Goldfish wrote; the LEGO story then forbade them.

**No identity anchor.** Conditioning every clip on the reference image as an extra keyframe suppressed the clip's closing action when placed at the end and inserted an idle beat when placed in the middle. On the correct recipe it is not needed.

**No re-seeding mid-film.** A cut re-seeded from the reference image resets everything the story changed: a cap picked up mid-film vanished, and a re-entry put a second figure on screen. Only a film's first clip is seeded from the reference; every other clip continues from the last 9 frames of the one before. After a server restart the client sends that tail, and the scene survives (a frame difference at the seam of 1.7, against 66 with a re-seed).

**Cuts are allowed, sparingly.** LTX-2.5 follows a written cut ("The view cuts to …"): 6 of 6 requested cuts happened and none of 12 unrequested. But the cut lands 2-4 s into the clip, a new angle renders as the figure turning, close-ups repaint the face, and some cuts dissolve. The LEGO story was one take; since every story may travel anywhere, and a walk does not reach a new place on screen (below), every story may now cut. In the first text film of a story with cuts on, Goldfish cut on 42 of 100 clips, mostly to a new angle; it is now told what a cut costs the render, and to cut only to another location or for a reveal.

**A place is drawn from where the protagonist is.** A location with a look is named in every clip's prompt (in full on the clip the picture arrives there, short while it stays), and the scene follows the protagonist: what is in the picture comes along, the rest stays where it was left. In the first 200-clip film of the quest story Goldfish moved the location 37 clips after the action entered the tower, so every prompt in between said "mountain pass" inside the tower. Told that the location is what the video model draws, and shown it in World now, it moved the location on the clip the picture arrived in a check film. On the GPU (an H200 NVL, one scripted chain and one live 120-clip film) a cut to a new place landed its look within 2.5 s, while a walk kept drawing the old place: one dissolved to near-black and the figure came back with a hair piece, another made the dragon's tower a beige castle hall. A story with cuts now arrives by cutting; one without cuts travels poorly. Wide landscape shots went photo-real; the story's style was then told that everything, landscape and sky included, is LEGO, and the story was retired before that was checked on the GPU. The H200 renders a 6 s clip in 2.8 s (p95 3.1 s), faster than the H100.

**No frame observer.** A vision model checked every rendered clip and could force a cut on drift. After the recipe fix it measured drift that was gone, its forced cut never fired in any run, and it misread framing. Removed.

**One labelled prompt compiler.** Caption-style prose prompts held identity no better and ran 22 % longer. Every shortened version of the visual anchor let hair pieces or printed shirts in by clip 12 on 2 of 3 seeds; the full anchor did on none. The compiler sent the full style and looks on the opening clip and a short anchor afterwards; since Frame Zero stopped carrying a style it sends looks only (see the Frame Zero entry).

**A prop keeps its look in every clip, and what a character wears is on screen.** A prop was described on the clip it entered and named alone afterwards, and the video model redrew it: a red lion shield turned grey, into a wooden door or a bronze disc within a few clips. In a scripted 14-clip chain on two seeds (the same seeds, only the prompt different), restating each prop's short look kept it recognisable in 43 of 54 checks against 22, and missing in 6 against 22. Goldfish also left worn and carried things out of `on_screen`, so the prompt never named them: a worn helmet was listed in 3 and 7 of 37 clips (28-64) in two live films, and Lego Man's own look ("bare yellow head") took it off. Told that the video model draws only what the prompt names, Goldfish listed it in 36 of those 37 and in 122 of the 123 clips of its life; the helmet stayed a helmet, but the picture put it on whichever figure was there (the dragon wore it from clip 125), because the prompt does not say who wears it. Characters still drift a little with their short look (a hair piece or hat in 7-9 % of clips), and wide shots still go photo-real; colour does not drift over 180 clips.

**A costume changes the wearer's look.** In a scripted chain with two figures (a herald hands Lego Man a helmet; two seeds, 22 clips each way), Lego Man wore it in 8 of 22 clips and the herald took it in 14, while every prompt still gave Lego Man a "bare yellow head". Saying where the helmet was ("worn on Lego Man's head") changed nothing (9 of 22), and every full look on every clip made it worse (2 of 22: the full look adds "no hair piece"); longer is not better, a look that agrees with the costume is. An op may now replace a character's or prop's look whole when its appearance changes for good: with the helmet written into his look Lego Man wore it in 20 of 22, but the herald grew a copy in 18 of 22, and keeping the herald's own cap in his look did not stop that. In a text film Goldfish wrote the new look when the helmet went on and the plain one back when a goat took it off; it also shortened the goat's look unasked, so it is told to leave `description` empty unless the look itself changes.

**512×288, one clip length.** One frame size, exactly 16:9, for the render and the reference image: identity and detail held by eye against 640×352 at 31 % less render time. One clip length (6 s), because under fp8 every new shape compiles for 90-170 s; the server warms the client's shape at startup and the client refuses to start without it.

**The video model renders all the sound.** LTX-2.5 renders ambient sound and the characters' lines from the prompt; an external narrator and TTS were removed, so every spoken word is a character's. Each clip renders 9 extra frames that repeat the previous clip's tail; the page cuts on them and crossfades the sound, with every clip held under -18 LUFS (no duplicate frames, no luminance dip). The model's own silences between clips still make audible steps; accepted. Two speakers in one clip render as two distinct voices (one scripted clip on an H200, judged by ear).

**No film is saved.** Each run used to join its played clips into one film (each clip's repeated 9-frame head shown once, the sound crossfaded: a 24-clip film saved exactly the 3,465 frames that played). Since the story is never replayed and the story folder keeps no video (above), clips are deleted once they have played and nothing is joined.

## Real time

The transformer's forward passes are compute-bound, so the levers were the GPU, low-precision matmuls, compilation and fewer tokens:

- fp8 matmuls (torchao, dynamic) and regional `torch.compile`: 3.0× to 1.9× real time at 960×544 on an H100.
- Fewer tokens: 640×352, then 512×288 (31 % faster again).
- Frames converted on the GPU and piped to ffmpeg as YUV with 8 x264 threads: render p95 0.89 to 0.67 of the clip's length.
- The client on the GPU box: an MP4 through an ssh tunnel cost 1.5-4.6 s per clip.

What did not help: the full model (3.5× slower, no better), FlashAttention-3 (refuses LTX-2's attention masks), NVENC (unavailable in Vast containers), offloading (28-57 s per clip). A 300-clip film showed no render creep; its three stalls all came from Goldfish calls of 6-14 s, which remain the main risk to a seamless film.

## Known open problems

- **Long films drift.** In the LEGO films the white void turned into a baseplate as props accumulated. Mac's story has not been rendered yet.
- **Continuity against steering.** The sliding window trades one for the other (`ltxv_continuity_strength`); a better mechanism is needed, not a better setting.
- **A quiet story in The White turns abstract.** With nobody typing, Mac's story becomes marks, doors and copies of Mac answering each other, and checkpoints stop; the prompts soften it, a concrete Frame Zero or a concrete want for Mac changes it (see "Text films of Mac's story").
- **Goldfish latency tail.** A slow call at a beat's end or on a line can still hold the picture.
- **Checkpoints stop in long stretches.** In a 240-clip film Elephant named one, at clip 86, and kept calling everything after it (15 places, two newcomers) one part, compressing it into a paragraph of 150-200 words; quiet 150-clip films named three, all in the first 60 clips.
- **Cuts feed on themselves.** Every version cuts rarely in its first 30 clips and on about a third of clips once cuts are in the recent clips Goldfish reads, mostly within one place, where a camera move would do.
- **Longer beats.** Goldfish's replies in the latest films ran 15-30 % longer than before (more lines and more ops), its median call 7-8.5 s against 6-7 s.
